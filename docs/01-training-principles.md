# 01 — Training principles

The methodology this system exists to serve. Every analytic in `04-analytics.md`
traces back to one of these.

## Governing constraint

**Systemic recovery is the ceiling** — not session length, not exercise count.
Evidence: a block where *all* lifts ran at 8 reps produced fatigue that forced
sessions to be postponed. Therefore development comes from *how* existing
exercises are loaded, never from adding more.

## Principles

**P1 — Progress through load structure, not volume.**
Session cap of 1–1.5h and a fixed exercise count per day are the frame, not an
obstacle. Levers: progression schemes, rep ranges, tempo, density, variant
rotation.

**P2 — The method is ramp-to-top-set, not pure double progression.**
Sets ascend in weight toward a target top set (e.g. front squat 70/80/90/100/110
× 6). The progression trigger is therefore implicit. Must be made explicit:
either fixed working sets, or a declared top-set target plus back-off sets.

**P3 — Explosive lifts do not belong at 6 reps.**
Snatch, clean, high pull, KB swings, jerk: bar speed decays by rep 5–6, training
"slow explosiveness". Correct zone is 2–3 reps, more sets, full recovery, stop on
speed drop. Already emerging organically (high pull logged as 90 × (2+2)).

**P4 — Vary DOWN (heavy triples), not UP (volume eights).**
For a recovery-limited athlete, volume blocks are the most expensive stimulus per
unit of fatigue. Cheaper variation: intensity + low reps + low set count. Keep
high-volume work as a small dose (2–3 slots per cycle), never as the general mode.

**P5 — Undulating beats block periodisation, for me.**
A block where every slot sits in a heavy volume range leaves no light session to
recover against, and ends in an involuntary deload. **Rule: never hold all slots
in the heavy volume range at once.**

**P6 — Different rep ranges are independent progress tracks.**
Progress on triples is separate from sixes and eights. A stall in one range does
not block the others. This yields several parallel growth signals on one lift.

**P7 — Hybrid load is additive.**
Total fatigue = strength + conditioning. Strength volume cannot rise without
taking something out of the metcons, and vice versa. Heavy and CrossFit days must
be programmed as one system, not two independent layers.

**P8 — Variety is bought with frequency.**
A long 12-day cycle with high variety means each specific movement is trained
about once per cycle. Linear progression works poorly at that frequency. Either
raise frequency of key patterns, or switch to a conjugate model ("beat my record
on *this variant*") rather than chasing linear +weight on a rare lift.

**P9 — Cycle length is near its practical ceiling (~12 days).**
Extending further pushes main-movement frequency below the useful threshold.
Future variety should come from the conditioning side via *formats* (AMRAP, EMOM,
For Time, intervals, ladders) and time domains — not from new movements.

**P10 — Planned deloads and retests are hygiene, not options.**
A postponed session due to fatigue is a planning failure, not a weakness. Deload
week at cycle end; periodic retest points (main-lift 1RM, benchmark metcons) as
progress anchors. RIR autoregulation (leave 2–3 reps in reserve on volume days)
as the guard against fatigue accumulation.

## What this demands of the data

- Set-level granularity, not session-level.
- Rep-band as a first-class dimension (P6).
- Movement *pattern* grouping, not just exercise name (P8).
- Pattern frequency per cycle (P8, P9).
- Combined strength + conditioning load per day (P7).
- Postponed/skipped sessions recorded explicitly as a fatigue signal (P1, P10).
- Deloads and retests flagged as anchor points (P10).
